A Very Brief History of Ypsilanti

Native Americans

Long before Ypsilanti was known as Ypsilanti, the area was inhabited primarily by the Potawatomi and Wyandot Indians, among other Native American tribes.○
Open citation.
 They built trails throughout the city, one of which is now Michigan Avenue (highlighted in yellow), and settlements just south of present-day Michigan Avenue
Jump to Edgenote
(triangle also highlighted in yellow).
By the 1830s, Native Americans were forcibly removed from newly incorporated City of Ypsilanti
Jump to Edgenote
and the larger Southeast Michigan area as White Europeans developed trading posts and villages in the area. Throughout this time and the decades that followed, Ypsilanti became critical settlement for Black slaves escaping the south along the Underground Railroad
Jump to Edgenote
.
Where these Black communities were allowed to settle isn’t surprising.

Black Americans

The vast majority of Black residents were segregated to the southside of Ypslanti
Jump to Edgenote
– south of Michigan Avenue – where the former Native American settlements were located.
Throughout the late 1800s and early 1900s, life for Black residents of Ypsilanti told a similar story to other Black Americans at the time – a story of segregation. Segregated homes. Segregated schools. Segregated restaurants. A former Black Ypsilanti teacher, Marguerite Eaglin, tells a story
Jump to Edgenote
of how segregation looked like during the 1950s in the city.
“…and the fact that because I was black, there were restaurants downtown that didn’t want us to come and eat. My children told me the story about how good Haab’s french fries were, but they had to stand outside and buy them from the window if they wanted any. I wasn’t aware of that. My friend Yvonne Williams and I would go downtown for lunch and we’d go into a place and sit and sit and sit. I was teaching school, so I only had so much time for lunch. Nobody actively insulted us, but they just didn’t wait on us. They just ignored us like we weren’t there. Finally in one place, I said, “Look, I have to go back to school. Do you know when we could get served, please?” They finally served us, but they burned the bread.” – Marguerite Eaglin, ca. 1980s
Jump to Edgenote
Stories like Marguerite Eaglin’s are important to understanding the lived experiences of residents in small cities. While her story tells us about the segregation she and other Black residents faced out and about in restaurants, others’ stories speak on the different dimensions of segregation. Could these stories uncover the legacy of redlining in Ypsilanti? Could they explain not only why Ypsilanti remains so racially segregated, but also the racial segregation in environmental pollution?
